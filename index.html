    Reading a Fischer projection

    • The carbon chain runs vertically with C1, the most oxidized end, at the top.
    • D or L depends only on the stereocentre farthest from the carbonyl: OH on the right is D, on the left is L.
    • You may rotate a Fischer projection 180° in the page. Rotating it 90°, or swapping any two groups, inverts the stereocentre.
    • D/L describes structure, not the direction of optical rotation: D-fructose is levorotatory (−).

      Stereochemistry concepts

      A compact reference for everything practised in this app. Stereoisomers have the same atoms connected in the same order but arranged differently in space.

      Chirality and stereocentres

      A molecule is chiral if it cannot be superimposed on its mirror image, the way a left hand cannot be turned into a right hand. The usual source of chirality is a stereocentre: an sp³ carbon carrying four different groups. A molecule with an internal mirror plane is achiral, even if it has stereocentres.

      Ranking groups (CIP rules)

      1. Compare the atoms bonded directly to the stereocentre. Higher atomic number wins: I > Br > Cl > S > F > O > N > C > H.
      2. If two are the same, list the three atoms attached to each, highest first, and compare item by item. The first difference decides. CH₂OH (O, H, H) beats CH(CH₃)₂ (C, C, H) because O beats C, even though isopropyl is heavier.
      3. Count a double bond as two single bonds: CHO carries (O, O, H) and CO₂H carries (O, O, O).
      4. If still tied, move outward along the highest-ranked branch and compare again.
      CH₂Cl outranks CO₂H: (Cl, H, H) against (O, O, O) is settled by the first comparison, Cl against O.

      Assigning R and S

      1. Point the lowest-priority group (4) away from you.
      2. Trace 1 → 2 → 3. Clockwise is R; counterclockwise is S.
      3. If group 4 points toward you, trace the path and reverse the answer.
      4. Swapping any two groups inverts the configuration.

      Enantiomers and diastereomers

      Enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images: every stereocentre is inverted, for example (2R,3R) and (2S,3S). They have identical physical properties except for the direction they rotate plane-polarized light and how they interact with other chiral things, such as enzymes and receptors.

      Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images: some stereocentres differ and others match, for example (2R,3R) and (2R,3S). They have different physical properties, so they can be separated by ordinary methods like crystallization or chromatography. Cis and trans alkenes are also diastereomers.

      Conformers differ only by rotation about single bonds. They are the same compound, so two models in different conformations can still be identical.

      Meso compounds

      A meso compound has two or more stereocentres but also an internal mirror plane, so it is achiral and optically inactive. It needs two halves with identical substituents and opposite configurations, as in (2R,3S)-tartaric acid. Because of the symmetry, (2R,3S) and (2S,3R) describe the same molecule. To spot a mirror plane, rotate about the central bond to an eclipsed conformation where like groups line up.

      Counting stereoisomers

      A molecule with n stereocentres has at most 2ⁿ stereoisomers. Meso forms reduce the count: 2-bromo-3-chlorobutane has four stereoisomers, but tartaric acid has only three, an enantiomer pair plus one meso compound. Glucose, with four stereocentres in its open chain, belongs to a family of 2⁴ = 16 aldohexoses, eight D and eight L.

      Optical activity

      Light is a wave whose electric field oscillates at right angles to its direction of travel. A polariser passes only the oscillation along one direction, giving plane-polarised light. A chiral sample rotates that plane; a second polariser, the analyser, is turned to find the new plane, and the angle is the observed rotation α. The intensity passed by the analyser follows Malus's law, I = I₀ cos²(θ − α).

      Chiral compounds rotate plane-polarized light: (+) or d for clockwise, (−) or l for counterclockwise. A 50:50 mixture of enantiomers is a racemic mixture and shows no rotation. The sign of rotation is measured experimentally and is not predicted by R/S or D/L: (R)-glyceraldehyde happens to be (+), while D-fructose is (−).

      • Specific rotation removes the effect of how much sample the light passes through: [α] = α / (l × c), with the path length l in dm and the concentration c in g mL⁻¹. It is quoted for a stated wavelength (usually the sodium D line) and temperature.
      • Enantiomeric excess (ee) is the percentage of the major enantiomer minus the percentage of the minor one. Because the two enantiomers' rotations cancel, ee also equals the observed rotation divided by the rotation of the pure enantiomer (the optical purity). A sample with 50% ee is 75% of one enantiomer and 25% of the other.
      • Mutarotation: a solution of pure α-D-glucopyranose ([α] +112.2) or pure β ([α] +18.7) changes until it reaches +52.7, the equilibrium mixture of about 36% α and 64% β.

      Reading and converting drawings

      • Wedge–dash: wedges point toward you, dashes away, plain lines lie in the page.
      • Fischer: horizontal bonds point toward you, vertical bonds away; the chain is eclipsed and runs top to bottom.
      • Newman: you look straight down one C–C bond; the front carbon is where three bonds meet, the back carbon is the circle.
      • Sawhorse: the same bond seen obliquely, with the front carbon at the lower left.
      • The safest way to decide whether two drawings show the same compound is to assign R or S to every stereocentre in each drawing and compare. Same configurations: identical. All inverted: enantiomers. Some inverted: diastereomers. Differences in conformation never matter.

      Symmetry and chirality

      The rigorous test is symmetry: a molecule is achiral if, and only if, it has an improper symmetry element. That can be a mirror plane (σ), a centre of inversion (i), or more generally an improper rotation axis (Sₙ: rotate by 360°/n, then reflect through the plane perpendicular to the axis; σ is S₁ and i is S₂). Proper rotation axes (Cₙ) do not make a molecule achiral: (R,R)-2,3-dibromobutane and trans-1,2-dimethylcyclopropane each have a C₂ axis and are chiral. The anti conformation of meso-2,3-dibromobutane has no mirror plane but has a centre of inversion, so it is achiral. Look for symmetry in any conformation the molecule can reach.

      E and Z alkenes

      Rotation about a C=C bond is restricted, so groups can be fixed on the same or opposite sides. On each alkene carbon, rank the two groups with the CIP rules. If the higher-priority groups are on the same side the alkene is Z (zusammen); if on opposite sides, E (entgegen). Cis/trans only works when the same group appears on both carbons, and it can disagree with E/Z: in (E)-2-chlorobut-2-ene the two methyl groups are cis.

      Conformations and Newman projections

      A Newman projection looks straight down one C–C bond. The front carbon is the point where three bonds meet; the back carbon is the circle. The dihedral angle is the angle between a bond on the front carbon and a bond on the back carbon.

      • Staggered conformations (bonds 60° apart) are lower in energy than eclipsed ones (bonds lined up). The extra energy of eclipsing is torsional strain: about 12 kJ/mol for ethane, or 4 kJ/mol per H/H eclipsing.
      • In butane, viewed down C2–C3, the anti conformation (CH₃ groups 180° apart) is lowest. Gauche (60°) is about 3.8 kJ/mol higher because the methyls crowd each other (steric strain). The eclipsed CH₃/H conformation is about 16 kJ/mol and fully eclipsed CH₃/CH₃ about 19 kJ/mol.
      • These barriers are small, so conformers interconvert rapidly at room temperature and cannot be isolated. They are not stereoisomers in the usual sense.

      Cyclohexane chairs

      Cyclohexane adopts a chair with no angle strain and every C–H staggered. Each carbon has one axial bond, parallel to the ring's axis and alternating up and down around the ring, and one equatorial bond pointing outward around the ring's equator.

      • A ring flip converts one chair into the other. Every axial group becomes equatorial and vice versa, but a group that points up stays up.
      • An axial substituent bumps into the two axial hydrogens on the same face, three carbons along (1,3-diaxial interactions). So substituents prefer equatorial. The energy cost is the substituent's A-value: about 7.3 kJ/mol for CH₃, so methylcyclohexane is about 95% equatorial at 25 °C. A tert-butyl group costs over 20 kJ/mol and effectively locks the ring.
      • For disubstituted rings, decide cis/trans by faces (both up or both down is cis), then work out which chair puts the most or bulkiest groups equatorial. Trans-1,2, cis-1,3 and trans-1,4 can be diequatorial; cis-1,2, trans-1,3 and cis-1,4 always have one group axial.

      Ring strain and small rings

      Rings are strained when their bond angles can't reach 109.5° (angle strain) or their C–H bonds are forced to eclipse (torsional strain). Strain shows up as extra heat released on combustion, about 658.6 kJ/mol per CH₂ for a strain-free ring.

      • Cyclopropane (strain about 115 kJ/mol) is necessarily flat, with 60° angles, bent "banana" bonds and every C–H eclipsed.
      • Cyclobutane (about 110 kJ/mol) folds about 25° ("puckered"), accepting slightly smaller angles (about 88°) to reduce eclipsing.
      • Cyclopentane (about 26 kJ/mol) would have nearly ideal angles if flat, but puckers into envelope and twist forms to relieve eclipsing. The pucker travels round the ring (pseudorotation).
      • Cyclohexane in its chair is essentially strain-free. Rings of 7 and 8 carbons are mildly strained again.

      Cyclohexane ring flip

      A ring flip passes from the chair through a half-chair (about 45 kJ/mol, the barrier), a twist-boat (a shallow minimum, about 23 kJ/mol) and the boat (about 30 kJ/mol, with eclipsed bonds and two clashing "flagpole" hydrogens), then back through a twist-boat and half-chair to the other chair. Every axial position becomes equatorial and vice versa, but groups stay on the same face, so cis/trans never changes.

      Fused rings

      In trans-decalin the second ring is attached through two equatorial bonds, and the bridgehead hydrogens are axial and anti. It cannot ring flip, because a flip would make both ring bonds axial and pointing in opposite directions. In cis-decalin the second ring uses one axial and one equatorial bond, so both rings can flip together. trans is more stable by about 11 kJ/mol. The two are diastereomers, and the bridgehead carbons are stereocentres. Steroids contain trans-fused rings, which makes their skeletons rigid.

      Cis/trans isomers in rings

      Two substituents on a ring are cis if they are on the same face and trans if on opposite faces. Judge chirality using the flat, time-averaged ring:

      • 1,2-Disubstituted rings (any size), and 1,3-disubstituted cyclopentanes and cyclohexanes: the cis isomer is a meso compound (achiral), and the trans isomer is chiral (an enantiomer pair). Three stereoisomers in total.
      • 1,3-Disubstituted cyclobutanes and 1,4-disubstituted cyclohexanes: the substituted carbons are not stereocentres, because both ways round the ring are identical. Cis and trans are both achiral diastereomers. Two stereoisomers in total.

      Fischer projections

      • Each cross is a stereocentre. Horizontal bonds come toward you; vertical bonds go away.
      • Rotating the drawing 180° in the page keeps the configuration. Rotating it 90°, or swapping two groups, inverts it.
      • To assign R/S: if the lowest-priority group (often H) is on a horizontal bond, the direction you trace is reversed. If it's vertical, read it directly.
      • For the aldoses and ketoses in this app, every stereocentre with OH on the right is R and with OH on the left is S, because the branch toward the carbonyl always outranks the branch away from it. That shortcut is not a general rule for other molecules.

      Sugars

      D and L. Look at the stereocentre farthest from the carbonyl group. OH on the right in the Fischer projection is D; on the left is L. Nearly all natural sugars are D. The enantiomer of D-glucose is L-glucose, with every stereocentre inverted, not just the last one.

      Epimers are diastereomers that differ at exactly one stereocentre. D-mannose is the C2 epimer of D-glucose, and D-galactose is the C4 epimer. Mannose and galactose differ at two centres, so they are diastereomers but not epimers.

      Aldoses and ketoses. Glucose (an aldose) and fructose (a ketose) are both C₆H₁₂O₆ but are constitutional isomers, not stereoisomers.

      Ring forms and anomers. In water, the C5 OH of a hexose attacks the C1 carbonyl to form a six-membered ring (a pyranose), creating a new stereocentre at C1, the anomeric carbon. The two possible configurations are anomers. In a Haworth projection of a D-sugar, the CH₂OH at C5 points up; groups on the right of the Fischer projection point down. The α anomer has the anomeric OH trans to the CH₂OH (down for a D-sugar); the β anomer has it cis (up). Anomers interconvert through the open chain, a process called mutarotation; for glucose the equilibrium is about 36% α and 64% β. In the chair form of β-D-glucopyranose every substituent can be equatorial.

      Amino acids

      • Every standard amino acid except glycine has a stereocentre at Cα. Glycine's side chain is H, so it is achiral.
      • D/L: in the Fischer projection with CO₂H at the top and the side chain at the bottom, NH₂ on the left at Cα is L, on the right D. Proteins are built from L-amino acids.
      • R/S: at Cα the priorities are usually NH₂ > CO₂H > side chain > H, so L-amino acids are S. The exception is cysteine: CH₂SH outranks CO₂H (S beats O), so L-cysteine is R. D/L and R/S are independent systems.
      • Two stereocentres: threonine and isoleucine also have a stereocentre at Cβ, giving four stereoisomers each: L-threonine is (2S,3R), L-isoleucine (2S,3S). The "allo" forms differ only at Cβ and are diastereomers.
      • CORN rule: with the H on Cα pointing toward you, CO₂H → R → NH₂ runs clockwise for an L-amino acid.

      Common mistakes

      • Ranking groups by total mass instead of by the first point of difference.
      • Forgetting to reverse the answer when the lowest-priority group points toward you.
      • Calling two conformers different compounds.
      • Assuming a molecule with stereocentres must be chiral (meso compounds are not).
      • Thinking R always means (+), or that D means dextrorotatory.
      • Inverting only the reference carbon when drawing the L enantiomer of a sugar.
